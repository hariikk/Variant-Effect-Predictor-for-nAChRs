Direction-Aware Variant Effect Prediction for Human Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptors: A Position Paper
Hari Krishna Mohan Raj* and Karl Kirschner Department of Computational Chemistry,
Hochschule Bonn­Rhein­Sieg, Sankt Augustin, Germany *E-mail: harikrishnam0711@gmail.com
Missense variants in human nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (nAChR) subunit genes are linked to congenital myasthenic syndromes, epilepsies, and a growing list of neurological disorders. Clinical sequencing now produces variants of uncertain significance (VUS) faster than functional assays can characterise them. Standard computational predictors report whether a variant is likely damaging but cannot distinguish loss of function (LOF) from gain of function (GOF), a distinction that directly determines therapeutic strategy. Recent tools such as MissION and LoGoFunc have demonstrated that direction-aware prediction is feasible for ion channels, yet none incorporate nAChR-specific structural biology or subunitlevel context. We argue that closing this gap requires (1) a curated, experimentally labelled dataset of nAChR missense variants, (2) a feature representation that captures subunit identity and structural context from cryo-EM data, and (3) a benchmarked machine-learning pipeline optimised for small, imbalanced tabular data. We present the architecture of such a predictor, discuss the design choices and challenges, and report results showing that engineered sequence and structural features carry measurable signal for separating LOF from GOF in nAChRs, and that subunit identity is the dominant predictor of direction. We conclude with a roadmap toward a clinically useful direction-aware VEP for this receptor family.
Keywords: Variant effect prediction; nicotinic acetylcholine receptor; gain of function; loss of function; ion channel; machine learning; position paper.
1. Introduction
Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) are pentameric ligand-gated ion channels that mediate fast synaptic transmission at the neuromuscular junction and throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems. Missense variants in the sixteen human genes that encode nAChR subunits give rise to a spectrum of disorders: congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) from muscle-type receptor mutations, autosomal dominant nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy (ADNFLE) from mutations in CHRNA4 and CHRNB2, and, increasingly, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric phenotypes linked to rare variants in additional subunits.11,12
The gap between variant discovery and functional understanding has become the central bottleneck in clinical genomics. Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing identify tens of thousands of missense variants per individual, but for the overwhelming majority the func-

tional consequence, and therefore the clinical significance, remains unknown; they are labelled variants of uncertain significance (VUS).a For nAChR variants in particular, the slow throughput of gold-standard electrophysiology (patch-clamp recording of recombinant receptors) means that fewer than five hundred variants have been experimentally characterised across all subunits, while population databases already contain thousands of observed missense changes in nAChR genes.
Existing computational variant effect predictors (VEPs) address one dimension of this problem: they estimate whether a variant is deleterious or pathogenic. Tools such as SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD, and AlphaMissense1­4 output a continuous or categorical damage score but provide no information on the direction of the functional change. This is a critical omission for ion channels and receptors, where both LOF and GOF can be pathogenic but demand opposite therapeutic interventions. A patient with a GOF nAChR variant causing ADNFLE may benefit from a channel blocker, while one with an LOF variant causing a myasthenic syndrome may require a potentiator or cholinesterase inhibitor. Collapsing both directions into a single "damaging" label erases the information needed for precision therapy.
A new wave of direction-aware predictors has recently emerged. funNCion6 targets sodium and calcium channel variants; LoGoFunc7 predicts LOF/GOF/neutral genome-wide using ensemble learning; PreMode8 employs graph neural networks for mode-of-action prediction; and MissION,9 the most recent entry, combines protein language model embeddings with structural features to predict direction of effect across 47 ion-channel genes. None of these tools, however, is built around the specific biology of nAChRs: the role of subunit identity, the distinct structural determinants at the agonist-binding interface and the channel pore, and the conformational coupling between the extracellular and transmembrane domains that underlies ligand-gated gating.
This position paper makes the case that direction-aware, receptor-specific prediction for nAChRs is both necessary and feasible. We contribute:
· A curated dataset of 717 experimentally characterised nAChR missense variants with LOF/GOF/no-net-effect labels extracted from primary electrophysiology literature (Sec. 4).
· An engineered feature representation that combines sequence physicochemical properties, evolutionary substitution scores, subunit identity encoding, conservation and proteinlanguage-model scores, and structural descriptors derived from cryo-EM structures (Sec. 5).
· A benchmark of ten machine-learning classifiers under leave-one-subunit-out crossvalidation, identifying gradient-boosted trees as the most suitable model class for this small, tabular dataset (Sec. 6).
· A critical discussion of the open challenges (data scarcity, the LOF/GOF spectrum, interpretability, and the absence of a shared benchmark) and a roadmap for the field (Sec. 8).
aThe American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines explicitly recognise that computational predictors alone are insufficient to resolve a VUS classification; functional evidence is required for upgrade to likely pathogenic.

2. Background and Related Work
2.1. nAChR Biology and the Clinical Importance of Direction
nAChRs are assembled as homo- or hetero-pentamers from a repertoire of sixteen subunits (1­7, 9, 10, 1­4, , , ). Muscle-type receptors (embryonic: (1)21; adult: (1)21) reside at the neuromuscular junction, while neuronal subtypes (predominantly 42 and 7) mediate synaptic transmission in the brain. Each subunit contributes a large extracellular N-terminal domain that harbours the agonist-binding site, four transmembrane helices (M1­M4) of which M2 lines the channel pore, and an intracellular loop that governs trafficking and modulation.10
The functional consequence of a missense variant depends not only on the chemical nature of the substitution but on where in the receptor it occurs. A substitution in the agonistbinding pocket may alter ligand affinity (typically LOF if it reduces binding, but GOF if it increases agonist sensitivity); a substitution in the M2 pore-lining helix may change channel conductance or ion selectivity; a substitution in the intracellular loop may affect receptor trafficking to the membrane (LOF) or desensitisation kinetics (potentially GOF if it prevents channel closure). Subunit identity further modulates these effects: the same substitution in 4 versus 7 can produce opposite phenotypes because the two subunits participate in receptors with different stoichiometries, expression profiles, and downstream signalling.
This complexity means that a predictor that treats all nAChR variants uniformly, without subunit context and without structural localisation , will systematically miss the biology that determines direction of effect. The central hypothesis of this work is that encoding subunit identity and structural context explicitly in the feature representation will improve LOF/GOF classification over sequence-only or position-agnostic approaches.
2.2. The Landscape of Variant Effect Predictors
Table 1 places this work in the context of existing computational tools. We distinguish three axes: (1) whether the tool reports pathogenicity only or direction of effect (LOF/GOF), (2) whether it incorporates ion-channel-specific knowledge, and (3) whether it models nAChR subunit identity and structure explicitly.
2.2.1. Pathogenicity-only predictors SIFT1 uses sequence conservation across homologues to flag substitutions that deviate from the evolutionary norm. PolyPhen-22 combines sequence conservation with structural features (surface accessibility, crystallographic B-factor) trained on HumDiv and HumVar datasets. CADD3 integrates over sixty genomic annotations into a single pathogenicity score using a linear-kernel SVM. AlphaMissense4 leverages AlphaFold's predicted structures and MSAbased conservation to achieve state-of-the-art proteome-wide pathogenicity prediction. 2.2.2. Direction-aware predictors
funNCion6 was developed for sodium and calcium channel variants associated with epilepsy and cardiac arrhythmias. It uses a random-forest model trained on 800 functionally characterised variants and achieves ROC  0.85 on held-out Nav variants. Its feature set includes Grantham distance, BLOSUM scores, and position within the channel protein, but it is not trained or validated on nAChR data.
LoGoFunc7 performs genome-wide LOF/GOF/neutral prediction using an ensemble of gradient-boosted trees trained on ClinVar labels and features spanning conservation, biochemical properties, and protein domain annotations. It reports balanced accuracy of 0.72 for the three-class problem, but treats all genes uniformly and does not encode receptor-specific structural context.
PreMode8 frames mode-of-action prediction as a graph-neural-network problem over protein structures, achieving strong performance on curated benchmarks. However, its reliance on high-quality structural data and its gene-family-agnostic design mean that nAChR-specific patterns, particularly those involving subunit interfaces, are not explicitly modelled.
MissION9 is the closest precursor to this work. It combines ESM-2 protein language model embeddings with structural features (secondary structure, relative solvent accessibility, perresidue B-factor) to predict LOF versus GOF across 47 ion-channel genes, reporting ROC-AUC of 0.925. MissION demonstrates that structural features complement sequence embeddings for ion-channel variant classification. However, it aggregates data across diverse channel families (Nav, Cav, Kv, nAChR, GABAA, etc.) and does not encode subunit identity or the pentameric assembly logic specific to nAChRs.

2.3. The Gap
No existing tool simultaneously (a) predicts direction of effect, (b) is trained or validated on nAChR data, and (c) incorporates subunit identity and pentameric structural context. This is the gap we aim to fill. We do not claim to outperform general-purpose tools on pathogenicity prediction; our claim is that for nAChR variants specifically, direction matters and existing

tools do not provide it.
3. Analytical Objective
We frame the problem as supervised classification over three functional classes: given an nAChR missense variant defined by its subunit gene, wild-type residue, mutant residue, and residue position, predict whether its effect is loss of function (LOF), gain of function (GOF), or no net effect (NNE). Because NNE variants are less abundant, we also report a binary LOF-vs-GOF framing that excludes them. The model is evaluated by its ability to recover the experimentally established direction of effect for held-out variants under leave-one-subunit-out cross-validation.
This framing deliberately excludes:
· Mixed-effect variants. A small number of variants exhibit both LOF and GOF characteristics (e.g., reduced surface expression but increased open probability). These are flagged in the dataset but excluded from training until sufficient examples exist for a dedicated analysis.
· Synonymous and non-coding variants. Only missense variants (single amino-acid substitutions) are considered, as they constitute the vast majority of clinically observed coding variants and are the focus of existing functional assays.
4. Experimental Data
4.1. Data Curation
We assembled a dataset of experimentally characterised human nAChR missense variants by systematic mining of primary electrophysiology literature. Search queries across PubMed, Europe PMC, and UniProt were designed for high recall, targeting papers that report patchclamp or two-electrode voltage-clamp measurements of recombinant nAChRs carrying single amino-acid substitutions. Each retrieved paper was manually curated to extract: the subunit gene, wild-type and mutant residues (three-letter and single-letter codes), residue position (numbered by signal-peptide-inclusive canonical isoform), LOF, GOF, or no-net-effect label as stated by the original authors, the experimental assay type, and the PubMed identifier. The complete set of primary source papers from which these labels were extracted is compiled in the References.16
The current dataset contains 717 variants (363 LOF, 190 GOF, and 164 no-net-effect) across all sixteen human subunit genes (Table 2), comprising 507 human, 138 rat, and 72 mouse variants (Table 3). The class imbalance (51% LOF) reflects the literature, where LOF variants are more frequently reported, likely because LOF phenotypes (myasthenic syndromes, severe epilepsies) are more readily ascertained clinically than GOF phenotypes, some of which may be subclinical or protective.

LOF and GOF labels follow the original authors' determination based on one or more of: (a) change in maximal agonist-evoked current amplitude, (b) shift in agonist EC50, (c) change in single-channel conductance or open probability, (d) altered receptor surface expression

4.3. Limitations of the Current Dataset
The dataset is limited in three important respects. First, with 717 labelled examples, it is small by machine-learning standards, constraining model complexity and raising the risk of overfitting. Second, labels are derived from heterogeneous assays performed in different laboratories under different conditions (oocyte vs. mammalian expression, different agonist concentrations, different temperature), introducing label noise. Third, publication bias likely means that variants with large, clear-cut functional effects are over-represented; variants with subtle effects

or effects only observable under specific conditions may be missing entirely. These limitations are common to all functional-genomics curation efforts and are not specific to nAChRs; we discuss mitigation strategies in Section 8.

5.1. Rationale by Category
Residue physicochemical properties. The chemical difference between the wild-type and mutant residue ( change in hydrophobicity, charge, size) is the most direct descriptor of a substitution's likely biophysical impact. A TrpGly substitution in the M2 pore-lining helix, for example, replaces a bulky hydrophobic side chain with a proton, drastically altering both steric packing and local electrostatics. We use established amino-acid index values from the AAindex database.13

Evolutionary substitution scores. BLOSUM and PAM matrices quantify how frequently a given amino-acid substitution is observed in aligned protein families, serving as a proxy for evolutionary tolerance. A substitution with a large negative BLOSUM62 score is rarely observed across evolution and is more likely to be functionally consequential; however, the sign of the score does not indicate direction (LOF vs. GOF).
Positional features. Where a substitution occurs within the subunit sequence determines which functional domain it affects. We encode normalised sequence position (residue index divided by total subunit length) and, where structural data permit, distance to functionally annotated motifs: the C-loop (agonist binding), the M2 pore-lining helix (channel conductance), and the M2­M3 linker (gating coupling).
Subunit identity. A 16-dimensional one-hot vector encodes which subunit gene carries the variant, complemented by a 3-way species one-hot (human/rat/mouse) in the positional block. This allows the model to learn subunit-specific patterns. For example, substitutions in CHRNA7 (a homomeric 7 receptor) have different typical effect directions from those in CHRNB2 (a neuronal  subunit that partners with 4).
Structural features. For variants that map to a residue resolved in an experimental cryoEM structure, we extract per-residue B-factor (a measure of local flexibility), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), secondary structure assignment (helix, strand, or coil via DSSP), and local packing density (number of neighbouring C atoms within 10 °A). We use structures of the human 42 and 7 nAChRs, and the Torpedo marmorata muscle-type receptor as a high-resolution proxy for human muscle nAChRs when human structures are unavailable at the relevant resolution.
We use one cryo-EM structure per receptor type: 6UW8 for the muscle receptor (CHRNA1/B1/D/E/G), 6CNJ for 42 (with CHRNA2 and CHRNA6 mapped by homology to the 4 chain), 6PV7 for 34 (with CHRNA5 and CHRNB3 mapped by homology), and 7EKT (closed) / 7EKO (open) for 7. For 9 and 10, for which no experimental structure is available, we use AlphaFold models (AF-Q9UGM1 and AF-Q13002). Variants whose residue is not resolved in the relevant structure are assigned a fill value, and the model is trained to degrade gracefully when structures are unavailable.
6. Algorithms and Experimental Design
6.1. Classifier Panel
We benchmark ten classifiers spanning a range of model complexity:
· Logistic Regression (L2-regularised). A linear baseline that tests whether the decision boundary is approximately linear in the feature space.
· Support Vector Machine (linear and RBF kernels). Linear and non-linear kernel methods, spanning both approximately linear and pairwise-interaction decision boundaries.
· Random Forest. An ensemble of decision trees that handles mixed data types and provides feature-importance estimates.

· XGBoost. Gradient-boosted trees with L1/L2 regularisation, widely used for tabular data competitions and biomedical classification tasks.
· LightGBM. Gradient-boosted trees with leaf-wise growth, often faster and more accurate than XGBoost on small datasets.
· CatBoost. Gradient-boosted trees with native categorical feature support and ordered boosting to reduce overfitting.
· k-Nearest Neighbours. A non-parametric baseline that classifies by local feature-space proximity.
· Gaussian Na¨ive Bayes. A probabilistic baseline that assumes feature independence, useful as a lower-bound sanity check.
· Multi-Layer Perceptron (2­3 hidden layers, ReLU, dropout). A simple neural baseline to test whether non-linear interactions beyond what trees capture are present.
6.2. Training and Evaluation Protocol
To obtain unbiased performance estimates on a small dataset where a dedicated hold-out set would be too costly, we use nested cross-validation with gene-level grouping:
(1) Outer loop: leave-one-subunit-out cross-validation, in which each fold holds out all variants of one subunit gene and trains on the remaining subunits (StratifiedGroupKFold with the subunit gene as the grouping variable). This guarantees that no variant from a held-out subunit leaks into training, the strictest test of generalisation to unseen subunits.
(2) Inner loop: stratified 3-fold cross-validation on the training split, used by Optuna14 for Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation (50­100 trials per model).
(3) Final evaluation: the model with the best inner-loop validation macro-F1 is retrained on the full training split and evaluated on the held-out subunit. Performance is reported as the mean and standard deviation of macro-F1, Matthews correlation coefficient, and balanced accuracy across outer folds.
Stratification preserves the class ratio in every split, and the gene-level grouping ensures variants from the same subunit never span both training and test. This is essential for nAChRs, where variants cluster heavily within a few subunits (e.g., CHRNA1, CHRNA7).
6.3. Baselines
We compare against three baselines:
· Majority-class baseline. Always predict the majority class (LOF). This yields macro-F1 0.224 on the three-class task and 0.396 on the binary task, with balanced accuracy 0.333 and 0.500, respectively. Any real signal must clear this floor.
· BLOSUM-direction heuristic. Predict GOF if the BLOSUM62 score is positive, LOF otherwise. This naive rule tests whether evolutionary conservation alone carries directional signal; it performs near chance.
· Leave-one-group-out ablation. The full classifier panel re-trained after dropping one feature group at a time. This quantifies the marginal contribution of each group (subunit identity, structural, substitution, etc.), as reported in Sec. 7.

7. Results
We evaluate the predictor on the final dataset of 717 curated variants under leave-one-subunitout cross-validation, in which each outer fold holds out one subunit gene and training proceeds on the remaining subunits. This is the strictest generalisation test available to us: models must predict effect direction for variants in a subunit they never saw during training. We report the three-class task (LOF vs no-net-effect vs GOF) as the primary framing, and the binary LOF-vs-GOF sub-problem (553 variants, obtained by excluding no-net-effect variants) as a secondary framing.

7.2. Ablation Analysis
To attribute performance to individual feature groups, we re-trained the models after dropping one group at a time (leave-one-group-out ablation) on the three-class task. The dominant contributor is the positional block (the one-hot subunit-gene encoding together with normalised

residue position and species). Removing it reduced three-class macro-F1 by 0.075­0.089 across CatBoost, Random Forest, and the linear SVM, confirming that subunit identity is the single most predictive input. Substitution scores (BLOSUM/Grantham) and the core structural block were the next most valuable. This directly supports the paper's central hypothesis: receptor-specific, subunit-aware context is precisely what a generic pathogenicity predictor lacks.
Adding conservation and ESM-2 zero-shot features on top of the 66 engineered features improved three-class macro-F1 by 0.023, but the binary GOF/LOF task improved by only 0.004. The two pathogenicity-oriented signals help mainly by separating the neutral (nonet-effect) class from functional variants, not by resolving GOF-versus-LOF direction. This is further evidence that direction of effect is a distinct problem from pathogenicity. Scaling ESM-2 from 150M to 650M parameters was a null result (three-class F1 -0.006; binary F1  0), so we retain the smaller, cheaper 150M model. The AlphaMissense score was likewise uninformative: dropping it did not reduce performance, consistent with its pathogenicity-only objective.
8. Discussion
8.1. Interpretation and Significance
The central claim of this position paper is that direction-of-effect prediction for nAChRs requires receptor-specific, subunit-aware modelling, and the results support it. The single most predictive feature block is subunit identity, and a leave-one-subunit-out evaluation, the setting in which the model must generalise to an unseen subunit, still clears the majority baseline by a wide margin. Gradient-boosted trees are consistently the best model class, as expected for a small, mixed-type tabular dataset.
Equally important is what did not help. Two of the most celebrated pathogenicity signals in the field (ESM-2 at larger scale and AlphaMissense) contributed nothing to the binary GOF/LOF task and only modestly to the three-class task. We interpret this as the core scientific finding of the work: direction of effect is orthogonal to pathogenicity. A variant can be damaging in two opposite ways, and features optimised to detect "damage" are by construction blind to the axis that matters clinically. Building direction-aware predictors therefore requires direction-labelled data and mechanism-aware features, not larger doses of pathogenicity modelling.
The absolute performance (macro-F1 0.51 three-class, 0.65 binary) is modest and below what would be required to drive clinical decisions on its own. It should nonetheless be read in context: the task is harder and the dataset roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than that of general pathogenicity predictors, and no prior tool predicts direction for nAChRs at all. The appropriate role for this model in the near term is as a triaging aid that flags whether a VUS is likely GOF or LOF, to be confirmed by functional assay.

8.2. Challenges and Limitations
8.2.1. Data Scarcity
With 717 labelled examples, the dataset is at least an order of magnitude smaller than typical training sets for general VEPs (CADD trained on 15M variants; AlphaMissense used 70M variants from population databases). Small datasets amplify three risks: overfitting, high-variance performance estimates, and limited generalisability to subunits or variant types not represented in the training data. We mitigate these through nested CV for unbiased evaluation, L1/L2 regularisation in tree-based models, and explicit reporting of per-subunit performance where sample size permits. But the fundamental solution, a larger dataset, requires community-wide curation efforts and, ideally, systematic functional characterisation of nAChR variants at scale.
8.2.2. The LOF/GOF Dichotomy
Biological reality is more nuanced than a binary LOF/GOF label. Variants can produce mixed effects (reduced surface expression but increased open probability), subunit-dependent effects (a substitution that is GOF in 42 but LOF in 7), or context-dependent effects (only observable under specific agonist concentrations or in the presence of allosteric modulators). Our binary framing is a practical simplification that enables supervised learning on available labels, but it should not be mistaken for a complete description of variant biology. Future iterations could adopt a multi-label or ordinal regression framework if data volume permits.
8.2.3. Label Noise and Assay Heterogeneity
Functional labels are extracted from studies spanning decades, laboratories, and protocols. Two-electrode voltage clamp in Xenopus oocytes (the most common assay) differs from patch clamp in mammalian cells in ways that can affect whether a variant is called LOF or GOF, particularly when the effect size is small. Systematic differences in how authors define and report LOF versus GOF introduce label noise that limits the achievable ceiling of any supervised model trained on these data.
8.2.4. Structural Coverage
Not all nAChR subunits have high-resolution cryo-EM structures, and not all residue positions within a solved structure are resolved. Variants in disordered loops or unresolved domains cannot be assigned structural features and must either be excluded or imputed from homologous structures, introducing additional uncertainty.
8.3. Future Work
The current score, while clearly above baseline, leaves substantial room for improvement, and we identify several concrete avenues.
Stronger sequence representations. Our hand-engineered substitution and conservation features are shallow compared with modern protein language models. Fine-tuning ESM-2

embeddings (rather than using only its zero-shot score), for example with parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA, and learning per-residue embeddings at the variant position may surface directional signal that zero-shot likelihoods miss. More expressive structure-aware models (AlphaFold2/3-derived residue contexts, thermostability change G from tools such as ThermoMPNN or RaSP) are also strong candidates for features that a pathogenicity-only score cannot supply.
Network and mechanistic features. Direction of effect often depends on where a residue sits in the pentamer and in the gating machinery: the agonist-binding C-loop, the M2 porelining helix, the M2­M3 linker, and the subunit interfaces. We plan to encode these locations explicitly from cryo-EM structures, and to explore graph-neural-network architectures (as in PreMode) that operate directly on the receptor structure rather than on flattened per-residue features. Network-embedding methods such as GenePlexusZoo offer a complementary, genelevel view of functional similarity that may transfer information across related subunits.
More data. The single most effective lever is a larger, cleaner dataset. We are extending curation across species (mouse and rat electrophysiology) and correcting residue numbering between species via sequence alignment, so that the cross-species augmentation already present in the architecture (138 rat and 72 mouse variants) can be fully exploited. LLM-assisted literature extraction targets the long tail of functionally characterised but not digitally curated variants.
Better modelling of the label structure. The binary LOF/GOF dichotomy is a simplification. We plan to move toward ordinal or multi-label frameworks that can represent the LOF/GOF spectrum and mixed-effect variants, and to add calibrated probabilities so that outputs can be interpreted as confidence estimates. An ensemble (stacking) over the diverse classifier panel may also yield a small but consistent gain, as the individual models make different kinds of errors.
Community infrastructure (longer-term). We advocate a shared, versioned benchmark dataset for nAChR direction-of-effect prediction, analogous to ProteinGym, to enable fair comparison across methods, and the extension of the approach to sister pentameric ligandgated channels (GABAA, glycine, 5-HT3). Ultimately, a direction-aware predictor could be integrated into clinical variant-interpretation pipelines (ACMG/AMP) to triage VUS and, longer-term, guide in silico drug repurposing by matching a variant's predicted direction to drugs with opposing mechanisms.
9. Conclusion
This position paper has argued that direction-aware variant effect prediction for human nicotinic acetylcholine receptors fills a genuine and consequential gap in the computational genomics landscape. Existing tools either report pathogenicity without direction or predict direction without receptor specificity; none combines directional prediction with nAChR subunitlevel modelling. We have presented the architecture of a predictor designed to bridge this

gap: a curated, experimentally labelled dataset; an engineered feature representation that encodes sequence properties, evolutionary constraint, subunit identity, and cryo-EM structural context; and a rigorous benchmarking framework under nested cross-validation with multiple baselines.
Our results show that the engineered features carry measurable signal for distinguishing LOF from GOF, that subunit identity is the dominant predictive input, and that gradientboosted tree methods are best-suited to this small, tabular dataset. Notably, celebrated pathogenicity signals (larger ESM-2 models, AlphaMissense) contribute little to direction prediction, underscoring that direction of effect is a distinct problem from pathogenicity. The main obstacles (data scarcity, the LOF/GOF dichotomy, label noise, and limited structural coverage) are real but not insurmountable, and we have outlined a roadmap for addressing them.
The broader ambition is not merely to build one more variant effect predictor, but to make the case that receptor-specific, direction-aware prediction is the right level of resolution for ion-channel variants and that nAChRs are a well-motivated starting point. We invite the biocomputing community to join this effort: contribute curated variant data, benchmark new methods against a shared nAChR test set, and help move variant interpretation from "damaging or not?" to "in which direction, and what should we do about it?"
